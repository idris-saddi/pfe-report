# General report rules

Mindset and universal rules a PFE reviewer applies, distilled from the template's
own commentary.

## The report is the face of your work

> Remember that your report is the face of your work: a bad report can overshadow
> excellent work. Pay it the attention it deserves.

The corollary: a great project, badly written up, scores worse than a modest project
clearly explained.

## Universal rules

- Writing has personal voice and signature, but some conventions are shared and
  universal — follow them, then add your own style.
- A computer-science project report has a fixed skeleton: TOC, Introduction,
  body chapters (theoretical / design / implementation), Conclusion, Bibliography,
  Appendix.
- Each chapter must have its own introduction and its own conclusion.
- Keep a clear narrative thread. The reader should follow a clean line of reasoning
  and see how the parts connect.
- Always highlight your contribution — it should be unambiguous and stand out.
- **Prefer full sentences over bullet lists.** From the original guide:
  > Avoid filling your report with bullet points -- favour sentences.
  Use bullets for genuinely list-like content (criteria, options, steps). For
  arguments, narratives, and conclusions, write prose.

## What the reviewer is looking for

- Can I see what the problem is?
- Can I see what you contributed (versus what was already there)?
- Are the design choices justified?
- Is the implementation credible (right tools, right diagrams, right artifacts)?
- Is the writing professional and free of basic mistakes?

If any of those are unclear, you lose points — independent of how good the actual work is.

## Proofreading is non-negotiable

> ALWAYS, ALWAYS have someone else (ideally outside the field) proofread your report
> for spelling and grammar.

An outside reader catches:
- Jargon you've stopped noticing.
- Sentences that made sense in your head but not on the page.
- Spelling and grammar mistakes your eye glides over.

## The "no titles at end of page" rule

> Never end a page with a heading.

Same for chapters: never end a chapter on a one-line orphan. Either rephrase or
push to the next page.
