% 1. CONTEXT
Contracts are the connective tissue of any business: they record what an
organisation has committed to, with whom, on what terms and until when. For
the European \textit{Mittelstand} -- the mid-sized enterprises that form the
economic backbone of Germany, Austria and Switzerland -- managing those
commitments has remained a stubbornly manual affair, even as the rest of the
back office has moved online. At the same time, the regulatory bar has risen:
GoBD\footnote{GoBD stands for \textit{Grunds\"atze zur ordnungsm\"a\ss igen
F\"uhrung und Aufbewahrung von B\"uchern, Aufzeichnungen und Unterlagen in
elektronischer Form sowie zum Datenzugriff}. It is a German administrative
directive that defines exactly how companies must handle, process and store
tax-relevant digital documents, to ensure auditability and prevent fraud.},
the European data-protection regulation (GDPR) and the
ISO~27001 security standard now expect business records to be retained
immutably, traced to an actor and a moment, and kept within controlled
boundaries. The discipline that addresses this, Contract Lifecycle
Management (CLM), is mature in the large enterprise, but the tools that
serve it there assume budgets, IT departments and rollout timelines the
mid-market does not have.

% 2. PROBLEM STATEMENT
The consequence is a recurring and costly gap. In a company without a
dedicated system, contracts are scattered across email inboxes, shared drives
and filing cabinets; renewal and cancellation windows pass unnoticed, so
agreements renew silently and decisions default to the counterparty; there is
no auditable record of who approved which version of a contract; and the
simple portfolio questions (how much value expires next quarter, where the
renegotiation leverage lies) can be answered only by a manual exercise that
is stale the moment it is finished. The platforms that could close this
gap either price and scope themselves for the enterprise, or, in the
compliance-aware DACH niche, require the customer to migrate every document
into the vendor's own storage, an entry fee that pushes mid-market buyers
back to spreadsheets and email.

% 3. CONTRIBUTION
This report presents \textbf{CLMPilot}, an integration-first contract
lifecycle management platform for the DACH Mittelstand, built during an
end-of-studies internship at Gradient~Zero. Its defining decision is to
manage the lifecycle of a contract (its metadata, deadlines, approvals and
audit trail) while the document itself stays in the customer's own storage,
so adoption carries no migration fee. The platform is built on \texttt{comby},
the company's in-house Go framework for Command-Query Responsibility
Segregation and Event~Sourcing, which makes the compliance-grade audit trail
a property of the storage model rather than a feature bolted on top. CLMPilot
was delivered as a release train, and the realised pilot (foundations, a
full-stack minimum viable product, and a tier of enterprise features) runs
in production at \url{https://clmpilot.com}. The remaining internship effort
then turns inward, from the product to the event-sourced system beneath it,
in a forward-looking engineering study of how that framework should evolve.

% 4. REPORT OUTLINE
The report is organised in two parts. The first frames the project and its
design. Chapter~\ref{chap:context} sets the general context and the scope of
the project: the hosting company, the market, the problem, the solution we
propose in response, and the methodology under which the work
was carried out. Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis} analyses and specifies the
requirements: the actors and the use cases they initiate, then the functional
and non-functional requirements that flow from them.
Chapter~\ref{chap:arch} presents the architecture and
design, justifying the choice of CQRS and Event~Sourcing on the \texttt{comby}
framework, deriving the domain model and the system topology, setting out the
security and compliance posture, and naming the seams the design deliberately
leaves open for later evolution. The second part covers realisation and
planned evolution: Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation} consolidates the work
delivered across the realised phases, and Chapter~\ref{chap:future} sets
out the planned improvements to the event-sourced system: extracting insight
from broker communications, deepening multi-tenancy, and scaling the framework
and the event store. A general conclusion then recaps the contribution and
opens the perspectives for the work that follows.
