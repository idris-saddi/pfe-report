\section*{Introduction}
This chapter establishes the context in which CLMPilot was built and the
scope the project set out to deliver. We introduce Gradient~Zero, the
hosting company that initiated the project, and characterise the market
the product addresses -- contract lifecycle management in the DACH
Mittelstand -- together with the regulatory, operational and commercial
pressures that make this market actionable today. From that context we
derive the four operational pain points the product responds to, and we
state, at the level of scope rather than implementation, the solution we
propose in answer to them. The chapter closes with the methodology under
which the work was organised: Extreme Programming, adapted to a solo
build and planned as a release train of product phases.

\section{Hosting company: Gradient Zero}
\label{sec:gradient-zero}

\subsection{General presentation}
Gradient~Zero\footnote{\url{https://gradient0.com/}} -- registered as
\textit{Gradient Zero Softwareentwicklungs~GmbH} -- is a software and
machine-learning company founded in 2018 and headquartered in Vienna,
Austria, operating under the banner of \textit{Machine Learning Excellence}.
Its core business is privacy-first artificial intelligence: it builds
platforms and bespoke solutions that let organisations extract value from
highly sensitive data without exposing it, and it develops parts of that work
into standalone products and spin-off ventures.

The company's technology portfolio reflects that focus. \textit{DQ0}
(Detabord) provides secure data management built on differential privacy, so
analysis can run over sensitive data in a GDPR-compliant way; \textit{Daiki}
is an AI-governance platform for managing the risks of trustworthy AI; and an
in-house private large-language-model and retrieval-augmented-generation
stack, a Git-based data-versioning platform certified by TÜV~Austria, and a
library of pre-trained machine-learning methods round out the offering. These
products are deployed chiefly in highly regulated sectors -- healthcare and
medical research, banking and financial technology, agriculture and industrial
automation -- where data-protection law is a hard constraint rather than an
afterthought.

CLMPilot, the subject of this report, belongs to the products-and-ventures
side of that portfolio. It is Gradient~Zero's contract-management offering,
and the most recent product built end-to-end on \textbf{comby}, the company's
in-house Command-Query Responsibility Segregation (CQRS) and Event~Sourcing
backend~\cite{Young2010,FowlerCQRS,FowlerEventSourcing}. The platform is
publicly available at \url{https://clmpilot.com}.

\subsection{Areas of expertise}
Of Gradient~Zero's technology assets, the one CLMPilot is built on is
\textbf{comby}, an internal Go framework that provides the building blocks of
a CQRS and Event~Sourcing application: command and query dispatch, an
event-store abstraction, projection runners, and a reactor scaffold. Comby is
the company's strategic answer to the build-versus-buy question: where each
new product would otherwise reimplement -- or import from an open-source
project -- the same plumbing for audit trails, approval workflows, and
temporal queries, comby makes that plumbing a shared asset reused across
products.

Around comby sits a broader engineering identity, visible across the
privacy-first portfolio and inherited directly by CLMPilot, that concentrates
on three threads. The first is \textit{compliance-by-architecture}: the use of
immutable event logs to satisfy GoBD and ISO~27001 requirements without
bolt-on audit features. The second is \textit{overlay-on-incumbents}:
products are deliberately not built to become the system of record for
customer data, but to layer workflow, governance and metadata on top of
the storage and applications the customer already uses -- a pattern that
runs through the company's privacy and data-governance products and that
CLMPilot applies to contract documents specifically. The third is
\textit{DACH-tailored productisation}: German-first user experience,
German tax-law constraints, and EU-hosted infrastructure as defaults
rather than premium options.

\subsection{Team and supervision}
The CLMPilot project was carried out as a solo build by the author of this
report, supervised by Mr.~Artur~Susdorf, Chief Technology Officer of
Gradient~Zero, through a weekly synchronisation meeting at the start
of each week. The release-train cadence detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} -- Phase~0 (foundations), Phase~1 (the MVP)
and Phase~2 (enterprise features), followed by a forward-looking
engineering effort on the event-sourced system itself -- was set jointly
with the company supervisor at the start of the internship and
adjusted in light of customer-discovery findings during the first
weeks.

Academic supervision was provided by Dr.~Aymen~SELLAOUTI, whose role
focused on the academic framing of the project: the soundness of the
architectural choices, the rigour of the methodology, and the structure of
this report.

\section{Project context: CLM in the DACH Mittelstand}

Contract Lifecycle Management (CLM) names the set of practices and tools
through which an organisation manages its contracts from inception through
negotiation, execution, performance, renewal and termination. While the
discipline is mature in large enterprises -- where dedicated CLM platforms
from vendors such as SAP, ServiceNow or Icertis are commonplace -- its
application to the mid-market has historically been thin. This section
sketches the market segment that CLMPilot targets, the regulatory pressure
that frames the opportunity, and the competitive landscape against which
the product's positioning was designed.

\subsection{The Mittelstand segment}
The German term \textit{Mittelstand} names the family of mid-sized
enterprises that form the economic backbone of Germany, Austria and
Switzerland -- collectively the DACH region. Definitions vary: the
Institut für Mittelstandsforschung classifies companies with up to 500
employees and revenues below 50 million euros as Mittelstand by its
qualitative criterion~\cite{IfMBonn}, while the European Commission's SME
definition stops at 250 employees~\cite{EUSMEDefinition}. For the
purposes of CLMPilot, the operative segment
is broader: companies of approximately 100 to 5{,}000 employees, generating
between 20 million and 1 billion euros in annual revenue, and operating in
industries where contracts are central to the business -- finance,
healthcare, manufacturing and professional services.

This bracket has two properties that drive product design. Contract volume
per company is substantial: typical customers manage between 200 and
10{,}000 active contracts at any time, with churn driven by renewal cycles.
And the operating model is mixed: most companies run on Microsoft~365 or
Google~Workspace for daily work, retain a small in-house IT function, and
have neither the budget nor the appetite for a six-month enterprise
software rollout.

\subsection{Market and compliance pressure}
Three structural pressures make CLM a present concern for Mittelstand
decision-makers rather than a deferred ambition.

The first pressure is \textbf{regulatory}. The German \textit{Grundsätze
zur ordnungsmäßigen Führung und Aufbewahrung von Büchern, Aufzeichnungen
und Unterlagen in elektronischer Form sowie zum Datenzugriff} (GoBD)
requires that electronic business records -- contracts among them -- be
retained immutably, with a verifiable audit trail, for periods of up to
ten years. ISO~27001 certification, increasingly demanded by customers in
B2B sales, imposes parallel requirements on access control and change
tracking. The European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) adds
data-residency and processing-record obligations on top of both.

The second pressure is \textbf{operational}. In the absence of a dedicated
CLM, contracts are scattered across email inboxes, network drives and
physical filing cabinets. Auto-renewal clauses trigger silently when
cancellation windows pass unnoticed, costing companies thousands of euros
per incident. Consolidated reporting -- \textit{``how much contract volume
expires in Q3?''}, \textit{``which vendors have we exceeded the agreed
thresholds with?''} -- requires manual aggregation across these scattered
sources, producing snapshots that are stale the moment they are delivered.

The third pressure is \textbf{commercial}. The global CLM software market
is projected to grow from USD~2.07~billion in 2026 to USD~5.09~billion by
2034, an 11.9\% CAGR over the forecast period, with North America
holding a 35\% share in 2025~\cite{FortuneBusinessInsightsCLM}.
Penetration in the DACH Mittelstand, however, remains low: the majority
of companies still operate on file-share-plus-spreadsheet processes.

Taken together, these pressures define a moving baseline: companies that
handled the issue informally five years ago are increasingly forced to
formalise their contract operations, and the tools to do so are demanded
today rather than \textit{eventually}.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

From customer-discovery interviews with target Mittelstand companies, four
pain points recur consistently, each with a distinct operational and
financial cost. The platform this report presents can be read as a
structured response to these four problems.

\subsection*{Missed deadlines}
The default outcome for a contract whose renewal window is unmonitored is
silent auto-renewal. A typical multi-year vendor agreement triggers a
one-year extension if it is not formally cancelled in a 30~to~90 day
window before its expiry; in practice these windows pass unnoticed because
no system is responsible for surfacing them. The financial cost per
incident varies -- from a few hundred euros for a low-value subscription
to tens of thousands for a vendor lock-in that should have been
renegotiated -- but the structural cost is constant: a critical business
decision is taken not by the people responsible for it, but by the default
behaviour of an external counterparty.

\subsection*{No audit trail}
When an external auditor or an internal compliance function asks who
approved a given contract, when, and on which version of the document, the
answer in the absence of a CLM is \textit{``let me search my email''}.
Email threads are not designed as auditable artefacts: they are mutable,
they degrade -- through deleted accounts and expired retention windows --
and they offer no guarantee that the version of the document approved is
the version currently on file. Under GoBD, this gap is sufficient to fail
an audit; under ISO~27001, it is sufficient to delay a certification.

\subsection*{No portfolio overview}
A common executive question -- \textit{``how much contract volume expires
in Q3?''}, \textit{``what is our renegotiation leverage with vendor~X over
the next six months?''}, \textit{``which suppliers will we exceed the
agreed thresholds with?''} -- has no good answer in a file-share-based
contract operation. The data exists, but its retrieval is a manual
exercise that consumes a person-week of effort and produces a snapshot
that is stale the moment it is delivered. Strategic decisions on supplier
consolidation, departmental budgets and M\&A diligence are routinely taken
with this information missing.

\subsection*{Approval chaos}
Contracts that should pass through legal review, financial review and
management sign-off -- in that order -- are in practice approved through
hallway conversations and forwarded emails. There is no enforceable
workflow, no record of who approved what, and no way to verify after the
fact that the approval policy was followed. The compliance exposure here
is severe: in a regulated industry, the absence of a documented approval
chain is, by itself, sufficient grounds for fines or for the invalidation
of the contract concerned.

\medskip\noindent
These four pain points are not independent. They share a common cause --
the lack of a system that treats the contract lifecycle as a first-class
object with its own metadata, deadlines, approvals and audit log -- and
they share a common solution shape, which the next section states as the
scope of this project.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Proposed solution}
\label{sec:proposed-solution}

We propose an integration-first contract lifecycle management platform
that answers the four pain points at their common root, by treating the
contract lifecycle as a first-class object. At its centre is a contract
registry that carries each agreement's metadata, deadlines, approvals and
audit history, and thereby gives the organisation the consolidated
portfolio view it lacks today. The documents themselves are deliberately
left where they already live: the platform links to files in the
customer's own storage rather than ingesting them, so adoption carries no
document-migration project.

Around that registry, three capabilities address the remaining pain
points directly. Automated reminders surface every cancellation, renewal
and expiry window before it passes, returning renewal decisions to the
people responsible for them. Enforceable approval workflows replace
forwarded emails with an ordered sequence of recorded decisions. And a
compliance-grade audit trail records every state-changing action
immutably, so that who approved what, when, and on which version of a
contract can be answered years after the fact.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The methodology under which CLMPilot was built was shaped by a single
operating constraint: the work was carried out by one developer against
a fixed set of phase milestones, with a scope that customer-discovery
findings were still refining during the first weeks. That constraint
also fixes the scope of this report. The internship delivered a working
pilot across three phases -- foundations, the MVP, and a tier of
enterprise features -- and then turned to a forward-looking engineering
effort on the event-sourced system that underlies the product. For the
build itself we adopted \textit{Extreme Programming}
(XP)~\cite{Beck2004}, an agile methodology suited to exactly these
conditions, in the solo variant described for a single
developer~\cite{C2XPForOne}. The subsections that follow define the
methodology, state how its base practices were applied, explain how its
team-oriented practices were adapted to a solo build, and describe the
release planning and traceability that organised the work.

\subsection{The Extreme Programming methodology}
Extreme Programming is an agile methodology introduced by Kent~Beck,
who defines it as a lightweight discipline of software development for
small teams facing vague or rapidly changing
requirements~\cite{Beck2004}. Its purpose is to lower the cost of
change: instead of fixing every requirement up front, an XP project
advances in short cycles, each of which leaves the product working,
tested and deliverable, so that feedback arrives while it can still
alter the plan. The methodology rests on five values -- communication,
simplicity, feedback, courage and respect -- and makes them operational
through a set of concrete practices, among them the planning game,
small releases, testing, refactoring, simple design, coding standards,
continuous integration, pair programming and an on-site
customer~\cite{Beck2004}.

\subsection{Base practices applied in this project}
Rather than survey the practices, we state how the decisive ones were
applied. \textit{Small releases} set the delivery rhythm: every release
leaves the platform in a deployable state, and the pilot reached
production early and was then re-released repeatedly, rather than
delivered once at the end of the internship. \textit{Continuous
integration} is enforced mechanically: each repository carries a
pipeline that lints, tests and builds every change before it can reach
a protected branch. \textit{Testing} is layered -- unit, integration
and end-to-end -- and the pipeline runs these suites on every change.
\textit{Simple design} translates into the deliberately narrow
technology stack motivated by the maintainability requirement, and
\textit{refactoring} keeps that design honest as understanding of the
domain improves. \textit{Coding standards}, finally, are held uniform
by linters and by conventional commit messages, which also carry the
traceability described at the end of this section.

\subsection{Extreme Programming for a solo developer}
Extreme Programming is written for teams, and some of its practices
presuppose a second person; the literature nonetheless describes its
use by a single developer~\cite{C2XPForOne}, and parts of the
discipline become simpler in that setting -- continuous integration,
for one, proceeds without merge conflicts. Two practices required
deliberate adaptation. \textit{Pair programming} is replaced by
asynchronous review: every change reaches the protected branches
through a pull request reviewed by the Gradient~Zero engineering team,
and the continuous-integration gate stands in for the partner who
keeps asking whether the tests still pass. The \textit{on-site
customer} is played by the company supervisor: each week opens with a
synchronisation meeting -- the project's planning game -- in which the
prior week's commits are reviewed against the roadmap and the next
items are selected, and architectural decisions are not on a fixed
schedule but raised as they arise. There is no fixed sprint length,
since a solo developer is best served by a continuous flow with
explicit checkpoints; the checkpoints are the release gates described
next.

\subsection{Release planning and traceability}
In Extreme Programming, release planning fixes what each release
contains and when it leaves. Stretched over the internship, our release
plan took the form of a release train of three product phases, each
with an explicit entry gate, exit milestone and demonstrable artefact:
\textit{Phase~0}~--~foundations (repository, CI/CD, development
environment, planning); \textit{Phase~1}~--~the MVP itself, covering the
contract registry, document linking, deadlines, approvals, dashboard and
IAM; and \textit{Phase~2}~--~enterprise features on top of the MVP
(intelligence-assisted extraction, electronic signature, per-tenant custom
fields, obligation tracking and advanced reporting). Each of these phases
advances the product to a state in which it can be deployed and used; no
phase is internal-only, and all three were realised and shipped to
production. The internship then moved past the product release train to a
fourth, different kind of work: a forward-looking engineering effort on the
event-sourced framework itself.

Traceability is enforced by three artefacts. The roadmap is the single
source of truth and is held in a Markdown document under version
control. A spreadsheet derived from that document tracks progress at
phase, section and subsection granularity, and is automatically
regenerated from the Markdown to keep the two in lock-step. The Git
history uses conventional commit messages, so that every change to
the code is attached to its motivating roadmap item by a uniform
prefix and a free-form description. Together these artefacts make it
possible to answer, for any line of code in the repository, the three
questions a maintainer asks first: \textit{what does it do, why is it
there, and which phase of the project introduced it?}

\section*{Conclusion}
This chapter has framed the work that the remainder of the report
describes. We placed CLMPilot inside Gradient~Zero's portfolio of
European mid-market products, characterised the DACH Mittelstand as a
segment, and showed that the regulatory, operational and
commercial pressures on that segment have made contract lifecycle
management an actionable need rather than a deferred ambition. From
that context we derived four operational pain points -- missed
deadlines, missing audit trails, absent portfolio overview, and
unstructured approval flows -- and answered them with the scope of an
integration-first platform that manages a contract's lifecycle while its
documents stay in the customer's own storage. Finally, we set out the
Extreme Programming methodology under which the work was carried out,
its adaptation to a solo build, and the release planning and
traceability that supported it.

With the context and the scope established,
Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis} refines them into a specification: the
actors the platform serves, the use cases they initiate, and the
functional and non-functional requirements the solution must satisfy.
