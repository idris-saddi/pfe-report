\section*{Introduction}
This chapter establishes the context in which CLMPilot was built. We
introduce Gradient~Zero, the hosting company that initiated the
project. We then
characterise the market the product addresses -- contract lifecycle
management in the DACH Mittelstand -- and the regulatory, operational
and commercial pressures that make this market actionable today. From
that context we derive the four operational pain points the product
responds to, the functional and non-functional requirements that flow
from them, the actors who interact with the platform, and the
project-management methodology under which the work was carried out.

The chapter answers four questions in order: who inducted the work,
what problem the product is designed to solve, what the product is
required to do, and how the work was organised. The architectural
response to those requirements -- the CQRS and Event~Sourcing framework,
the domain decomposition and the deployment topology -- is the subject
of Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}. The realised platform that resulted -- the
engineering foundations, the contract-lifecycle core and the enterprise
capabilities delivered -- is then consolidated by
engineering theme in Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation}, and the forward-looking
engineering work that the remainder of the internship is devoted to --
improvements to the event-sourced system itself -- is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chap:future}.

\section{Hosting company: Gradient Zero}

\subsection{General presentation}
Gradient~Zero\footnote{\url{https://gradient0.com/}} -- registered as
\textit{Gradient Zero Softwareentwicklungs~GmbH} -- is a software and
machine-learning company founded in 2018 and headquartered in Vienna,
Austria, operating under the banner of \textit{Machine Learning Excellence}.
Its core business is privacy-first artificial intelligence: it builds
platforms and bespoke solutions that let organisations extract value from
highly sensitive data without exposing it, and it develops parts of that work
into standalone products and spin-off ventures.

The company's technology portfolio reflects that focus. \textit{DQ0}
(Detabord) provides secure data management built on differential privacy, so
analysis can run over sensitive data in a GDPR-compliant way; \textit{Daiki}
is an AI-governance platform for managing the risks of trustworthy AI; and an
in-house private large-language-model and retrieval-augmented-generation
stack, a Git-based data-versioning platform certified by TÜV~Austria, and a
library of pre-trained machine-learning methods round out the offering. These
products are deployed chiefly in highly regulated sectors -- healthcare and
medical research, banking and financial technology, agriculture and industrial
automation -- where data-protection law is a hard constraint rather than an
afterthought.

CLMPilot, the subject of this report, belongs to the products-and-ventures
side of that portfolio. It is Gradient~Zero's contract-management offering,
and the most recent product built end-to-end on \textbf{comby}, the company's
in-house Command-Query Responsibility Segregation (CQRS) and Event~Sourcing
backend~\cite{Young2010,FowlerCQRS,FowlerEventSourcing}. The platform is
publicly available at \url{https://clmpilot.com}.

\subsection{Areas of expertise}
Of Gradient~Zero's technology assets, the one CLMPilot is built on is
\textbf{comby}, an internal Go framework that provides the building blocks of
a CQRS and Event~Sourcing application: command and query dispatch, an
event-store abstraction, projection runners, and a reactor scaffold. Comby is
the company's strategic answer to the build-versus-buy question: where each
new product would otherwise reimplement -- or import from an open-source
project -- the same plumbing for audit trails, approval workflows, and
temporal queries, comby makes that plumbing a shared asset reused across
products.

Around comby sits a broader engineering identity, visible across the
privacy-first portfolio and inherited directly by CLMPilot, that concentrates
on three threads. The first is \textit{compliance-by-architecture}: the use of
immutable event logs to satisfy GoBD and ISO~27001 requirements without
bolt-on audit features. The second is \textit{overlay-on-incumbents}:
products are deliberately not built to become the system of record for
customer data, but to layer workflow, governance and metadata on top of
the storage and applications the customer already uses -- a pattern that
runs through the company's privacy and data-governance products and that
CLMPilot applies to contract documents specifically. The third is
\textit{DACH-tailored productisation}: German-first user experience,
German tax-law constraints, and EU-hosted infrastructure as defaults
rather than premium options.

\section{Project context: CLM in the DACH Mittelstand}

Contract Lifecycle Management (CLM) names the set of practices and tools
through which an organisation manages its contracts from inception through
negotiation, execution, performance, renewal and termination. While the
discipline is mature in large enterprises -- where dedicated CLM platforms
from vendors such as SAP, ServiceNow or Icertis are commonplace -- its
application to the mid-market has historically been thin. This section
sketches the market segment that CLMPilot targets, the regulatory pressure
that frames the opportunity, and the competitive landscape against which
the product's positioning was designed.

\subsection{The Mittelstand segment}
The German term \textit{Mittelstand} names the family of mid-sized
enterprises that form the economic backbone of Germany, Austria and
Switzerland -- collectively the DACH region. Definitions vary: the
Institut für Mittelstandsforschung classifies companies with up to 500
employees and revenues below 50 million euros as Mittelstand by its
qualitative criterion~\cite{IfMBonn}, while the European Commission's SME
definition stops at 250 employees~\cite{EUSMEDefinition}. For the
purposes of CLMPilot, the operative segment
is broader: companies of approximately 100 to 5{,}000 employees, generating
between 20 million and 1 billion euros in annual revenue, and operating in
industries where contracts are central to the business -- finance,
healthcare, manufacturing and professional services.

This bracket has two properties that drive product design. Contract volume
per company is substantial: typical customers manage between 200 and
10{,}000 active contracts at any time, with churn driven by renewal cycles.
And the operating model is mixed: most companies run on Microsoft~365 or
Google~Workspace for daily work, retain a small in-house IT function, and
have neither the budget nor the appetite for a six-month enterprise
software rollout.

\subsection{Market and compliance pressure}
Three structural pressures make CLM a present concern for Mittelstand
decision-makers rather than a deferred ambition.

The first pressure is \textbf{regulatory}. The German \textit{Grundsätze
zur ordnungsmäßigen Führung und Aufbewahrung von Büchern, Aufzeichnungen
und Unterlagen in elektronischer Form sowie zum Datenzugriff} (GoBD)
requires that electronic business records -- contracts among them -- be
retained immutably, with a verifiable audit trail, for periods of up to
ten years. ISO~27001 certification, increasingly demanded by customers in
B2B sales, imposes parallel requirements on access control and change
tracking. The European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) adds
data-residency and processing-record obligations on top of both.

The second pressure is \textbf{operational}. In the absence of a dedicated
CLM, contracts are scattered across email inboxes, network drives and
physical filing cabinets. Auto-renewal clauses trigger silently when
cancellation windows pass unnoticed, costing companies thousands of euros
per incident. Consolidated reporting -- \textit{``how much contract volume
expires in Q3?''}, \textit{``which vendors have we exceeded the agreed
thresholds with?''} -- requires manual aggregation across these scattered
sources, producing snapshots that are stale the moment they are delivered.

The third pressure is \textbf{commercial}. The global CLM software market
is projected to grow from USD~2.07~billion in 2026 to USD~5.09~billion by
2034, an 11.9\% CAGR over the forecast period, with North America
holding a 35\% share in 2025~\cite{FortuneBusinessInsightsCLM}.
Penetration in the DACH Mittelstand, however, remains low: the majority
of companies still operate on file-share-plus-spreadsheet processes.

Taken together, these pressures define a moving baseline: companies that
handled the issue informally five years ago are increasingly forced to
formalise their contract operations, and the tools to do so are demanded
today rather than \textit{eventually}.

\section{Problem statement}

From customer-discovery interviews with target Mittelstand companies, four
pain points recur consistently, each with a distinct operational and
financial cost. The architecture presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}, 
and the realised platform consolidated in Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation},
can be read as a structured response to these four problems.

\subsection*{Missed deadlines}
The default outcome for a contract whose renewal window is unmonitored is
silent auto-renewal. A typical multi-year vendor agreement triggers a
one-year extension if it is not formally cancelled in a 30~to~90 day
window before its expiry; in practice these windows pass unnoticed because
no system is responsible for surfacing them. The financial cost per
incident varies -- from a few hundred euros for a low-value subscription
to tens of thousands for a vendor lock-in that should have been
renegotiated -- but the structural cost is constant: a critical business
decision is taken not by the people responsible for it, but by the default
behaviour of an external counterparty.

\subsection*{No audit trail}
When an external auditor or an internal compliance function asks who
approved a given contract, when, and on which version of the document, the
answer in the absence of a CLM is \textit{``let me search my email''}.
Email threads are not designed as auditable artefacts: they are mutable,
they degrade -- through deleted accounts and expired retention windows --
and they offer no guarantee that the version of the document approved is
the version currently on file. Under GoBD, this gap is sufficient to fail
an audit; under ISO~27001, it is sufficient to delay a certification.

\subsection*{No portfolio overview}
A common executive question -- \textit{``how much contract volume expires
in Q3?''}, \textit{``what is our renegotiation leverage with vendor~X over
the next six months?''}, \textit{``which suppliers will we exceed the
agreed thresholds with?''} -- has no good answer in a file-share-based
contract operation. The data exists, but its retrieval is a manual
exercise that consumes a person-week of effort and produces a snapshot
that is stale the moment it is delivered. Strategic decisions on supplier
consolidation, departmental budgets and M\&A diligence are routinely taken
with this information missing.

\subsection*{Approval chaos}
Contracts that should pass through legal review, financial review and
management sign-off -- in that order -- are in practice approved through
hallway conversations and forwarded emails. There is no enforceable
workflow, no record of who approved what, and no way to verify after the
fact that the approval policy was followed. The compliance exposure here
is severe: in a regulated industry, the absence of a documented approval
chain is, by itself, sufficient grounds for fines or for the invalidation
of the contract concerned.

\medskip\noindent
These four pain points are not independent. They share a common cause --
the lack of a system that treats the contract lifecycle as a first-class
object with its own metadata, deadlines, approvals and audit log -- and
they share a common solution shape, which is the platform presented in the
chapters that follow.

\section{Functional requirements}

The functional requirements that follow flow directly from the four pain
points of the previous section. They are stated at the level of capability,
not implementation: each subsection identifies the actor that initiates
the use case, the outcome the platform must produce, and the constraints
the surrounding context imposes. The architectural decomposition into
domains, commands and events is deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}.

\subsection{Contract registry}
The contract registry is the platform's central object and the entry
point for every other capability. The Legal~Manager creates a contract
record by supplying the counterparty, the contract category, the start
and end dates, the notice window and the contract value. The same actor
edits these attributes over the contract's lifetime and decommissions
the record by archiving rather than deleting it -- the immutability
requirement of Section~\ref{sec:nfr-audit} forbids hard deletion. The
registry supports filtering by status, category, business unit and
counterparty, full-text search on free-form fields, and pagination over
the result set. Performance targets for these operations are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:nfr-perf}.

\subsection{Document linking}
A contract record is connected to the document that materialises it
through a \textit{link} -- a typed reference to a file held in the
customer's own document store (Google~Drive, SharePoint, or any
HTTP-addressable location). The Legal~Manager attaches and detaches
links; the platform resolves them at display time, surfacing the file
name, last-modified timestamp and a click-through to the original
location. The platform never copies the document contents into its own
storage: the integration-first stance of
Section~\ref{sec:competitive-landscape} is enforced at this boundary.

\subsection{Deadline and renewal management}
Every contract carries up to three temporal markers: its expiry date,
the start of its notice window, and -- where applicable -- its
auto-renewal date. The platform must surface each of these markers
before it passes, through a reminder workflow that emits notifications
at 90, 60 and 30~days before the notice window opens, and again on the
day the window closes. The notification target is the contract's
designated owner, by default the Legal~Manager who created the record.
The reminder schedule is generated by the platform itself: the actor
who triggers the use case is the system clock, not a human user.

\subsection{Approval workflows}
A contract under negotiation moves through a sequence of approvals
before it is signed. The Legal~Manager initiates an approval request
and selects a workflow template -- a sequence of approval steps, each
addressed to one or several Approvers. Each Approver receives a request
and answers with one of three decisions: approve, reject, or request
changes. A reject or request-changes outcome interrupts the workflow
and returns the contract to the initiator; an approval advances it to
the next step. The platform records every decision, its author, its
timestamp and any comment, and these records are not editable after
the fact.

\subsection{Compliance and audit trail}
Every action that changes the state of a contract -- creation, edit,
link attachment, approval decision, archiving -- is recorded in an
immutable audit log. The Auditor accesses the log read-only and exports
it in a format that survives the application: a tabular file usable in
spreadsheet software, with one row per recorded action, and columns for
the actor, the timestamp, the contract identifier and the action type.
The log must satisfy the GoBD retention requirement of ten years and
must be reproducible at any date within that window, even after the
underlying business state has changed.

\subsection{Dashboard and reporting}
The dashboard is the Legal~Manager's daily view. It surfaces the
portfolio at a glance: counts of active, expired and pending contracts;
the contracts whose notice windows open in the next 30, 60 and 90~days;
the approval requests awaiting the user's decision; and the aggregate
value of the active portfolio. Beyond the standing dashboard, the
platform supports parametric reports -- contracts by counterparty,
spend by quarter, renewals by business unit -- to answer the kind of
question the problem statement identifies as routinely unanswered.

\subsection{Identity and access management}
Identity and access management (IAM) is a precondition for every other
capability. The Admin creates user accounts, assigns roles and revokes
access; users authenticate with an email address, a password and a
second factor delivered as a one-time code by email. The platform enforces role-based access
control along the actor catalogue of
Section~\ref{sec:actors-catalogue}: the same query can return different
projections depending on the caller's role, and any command that
exceeds the caller's authority is refused before it reaches the domain.
The reset, invitation and lockout flows are required at this layer
because they are the everyday surface through which IAM is exercised
by non-Admin users.

\subsection{Enterprise capabilities}
\label{sec:fr-enterprise}
Beyond the core registry, the platform is required to support a tier of
enterprise capabilities that deepen the response to the same four pain
points. Document linking is expected to trigger \textit{intelligence-assisted
capture}: when a contract's document is attached, the platform proposes
counterparty, dates, value and detected clauses for the Legal~Manager to
confirm, rather than leaving every field to manual entry. The platform must
take a contract through \textit{electronic signature} with an external
counterparty and reflect the signed outcome on the record, so that execution
is part of the tracked lifecycle rather than an out-of-band step.
\textit{Per-tenant custom fields} let each organisation extend the contract
schema to its own taxonomy without a code change. \textit{Post-signature
obligation tracking} expands standard deadline management, ensuring the 
organization monitors and fulfills all operational deliverables throughout 
the active lifecycle of the contract. And \textit{advanced, schedulable 
reporting} answers the portfolio questions of the problem statement on a 
recurring basis rather than on demand. These capabilities are stated here 
as requirements; their realisation is consolidated in Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation}.

\section{Non-functional requirements}

Non-functional requirements describe the qualities the platform must
exhibit while delivering the functional capabilities of the previous
section. We group them along seven axes -- security, compliance,
availability, performance, auditability, internationalisation and
maintainability -- each justified by a constraint of the deployment
context rather than by a feature in isolation.

\subsection{Security}
\label{sec:nfr-security}
All traffic between the client and the platform is carried over
Transport-Layer Security (TLS) in its current widely-deployed version;
data at rest in the event store and the relational projections is
encrypted with a key under the operator's control. Every command and
query is authenticated; authorisation is enforced before the request
reaches the domain layer, on the basis of the caller's role. Passwords
are stored as salted hashes computed with a modern key-derivation
function. A second factor of authentication -- a one-time code delivered by
email -- is mandatory for Admin accounts and offered to every other
role.

\subsection{Compliance and data residency}
\label{sec:nfr-compliance}
The platform must satisfy three regulatory baselines simultaneously.
GoBD requires that contract records be retained immutably and
recoverable in their state as of any past date, for a period of up to
ten years. The GDPR requires that
personal data be processed under documented purposes, subject to access
and erasure requests, and stored within the European Union. ISO~27001
imposes a documented information-security management system, with
access controls, change tracking and incident response procedures. The
production environment is hosted in the European Union by default,
under a contract with an EU-resident provider; no customer data is
processed or stored outside the EU without an explicit opt-in.

\subsection{Availability and reliability}
The minimum viable product (MVP) carries an availability target rather
than a contractually committed Service-Level Objective (SLO):
99.5\% monthly during DACH business hours and
99.0\% outside them, to be validated against operational
data once production traffic is observed. Recovery-Point Objective
(RPO) is set at 24 hours, sustained by nightly off-site
backups; Recovery-Time Objective (RTO) at 4 hours. These figures
are MVP targets and will be tightened or relaxed based on customer
commitments in later phases. The platform degrades gracefully when
external document stores are unreachable: contract metadata and 
workflow remain available, and document previews surface a 
transient-error state rather than failing the surrounding page.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:nfr-perf}
The performance budget for the MVP is expressed as engineering
targets to be measured and tuned against, not as contractual
guarantees. On a contemporary EU broadband connection, median
page-load latency aims for under one second on the dashboard and the
contract list; search and filter operations over a portfolio of ten
thousand contracts aim for under five hundred milliseconds; and
document-link resolution -- the round-trip to the external store,
plus the metadata fetch -- aims for under two seconds in the median
case. Failure modes degrade to the graceful state described above
rather than blocking the user interface.

\subsection{Auditability}
\label{sec:nfr-audit}
Every state-changing action in the platform is recorded as an immutable,
time-stamped, actor-attributed event. There is no primitive for
in-place update or destructive delete at the storage layer: a correction
is itself an event, layered on top of the prior history. The audit log
is exportable in a tabular format independent of the application's
runtime and is reconstructible at any point in time within the GoBD
retention window. This requirement aligns directly with the
compliance-by-architecture and shapes the architectural choices 
presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}.

\subsection{Internationalisation}
\label{sec:nfr-i18n}
English is the primary language of the user interface, with German
maintained alongside it; Other languages could be targeted for a 
future expansion, with translation slots reserved in the user-interface 
layer from the outset. Dates, numbers and currencies are formatted 
according to the user's declared locale rather than the server's, 
and the platform stores time-stamps in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) 
and renders them in the user's time zone. Email notifications inherit 
the recipient's locale.

\subsection{Maintainability}
The platform was built by a single developer during a brief internship, 
every architectural choice had to minimize the long-term maintenance burden. 
The platform deliberately relies on a narrow, well-understood technology 
stack rather than chasing a fragmented mix of "best tools". 
To keep the project manageable for future developers, it incorporates 
conventional commit messages, a predictable release-train cadence 
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), and three distinct layers of automated 
testing (unit, integration, and end-to-end). Finally, documentation is stored 
directly alongside the code and compiled straight into the running application's 
help system, preventing the docs from drifting out of date.

role belongs to the operator rather than the customer, and is treated

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The methodology under which CLMPilot was built was shaped by a single
operating constraint: the work was carried out by one developer
against a fixed set of phase milestones. That constraint also fixes the
scope of this report. The internship delivered a working pilot across
three phases -- foundations, the MVP, and a tier of enterprise features --
and then turned to a forward-looking engineering effort on the
event-sourced system that underlies the product. The two
subsections that follow describe the delivery model that produced the
pilot -- a release train with absolute phase gates -- and the planning
cadence and traceability artefacts that supported it.

\subsection{Release-train delivery}
The build was organised as a release train of three product phases, each
with an explicit entry gate, exit milestone and demonstrable artefact:
\textit{Phase~0}~--~foundations (repository, CI/CD, development
environment, planning); \textit{Phase~1}~--~the MVP itself, covering the
contract registry, document linking, deadlines, approvals, dashboard and
IAM; and \textit{Phase~2}~--~enterprise features on top of the MVP
(intelligence-assisted extraction, electronic signature, per-tenant custom
fields, obligation tracking and advanced reporting). Each of these phases
advances the product to a state in which it can be deployed and used; no
phase is internal-only, and all three were realised and shipped to
production. The internship then moved past the product release train to a
fourth, different kind of work: a forward-looking engineering effort on the
event-sourced framework itself.
Figure~\ref{fig:release-train} shows the three realised phases, the gates
between them, and the evolution that follows.

\subsection{Planning cadence and traceability}
The planning cadence is weekly. Each week opens with a synchronisation
meeting with the company supervisor, in which the prior week's
commits are reviewed against the roadmap and the next items are
selected. Architectural decisions are not on a fixed schedule: they
are raised as they arise and discussed with the supervisor at the
next available opportunity. There is no fixed sprint length, since a
solo developer working to a release-train schedule is best served by
a continuous flow with explicit checkpoints.

Traceability is enforced by three artefacts. The roadmap is the single
source of truth and is held in a Markdown document under version
control. A spreadsheet derived from that document tracks progress at
phase, section and subsection granularity, and is automatically
regenerated from the Markdown to keep the two in lock-step. The Git
history uses conventional commit messages, so that every change to
the code is attached to its motivating roadmap item by a uniform
prefix and a free-form description. Together these artefacts make it
possible to answer, for any line of code in the repository, the three
questions a maintainer asks first: \textit{what does it do, why is it
there, and which phase of the project introduced it?}

\section*{Conclusion}
This chapter has framed the work that the remainder of the report
describes. We placed CLMPilot inside Gradient~Zero's portfolio of
European mid-market products, characterised the DACH Mittelstand as a
segment, and showed that the regulatory, operational and
commercial pressures on that segment have made contract lifecycle
management an actionable need rather than a deferred ambition. From
that context we derived four operational pain points -- missed
deadlines, missing audit trails, absent portfolio overview, and
unstructured approval flows -- and the functional and non-functional
requirements that flow from them. We catalogued the platform's actors --
four per-tenant human roles specialising a common user, together with the
system clock that drives its scheduled actions -- and expanded the use
cases they initiate from a single high-level overview down to the
individual cases of each functional area. Finally, we set out the
release-train methodology under which the work was carried out.

With the context and the constraints established, the next chapter
turns to the structural response. Chapter~\ref{chap:arch} presents the
CQRS and Event~Sourcing architecture, the comby framework on which
CLMPilot is built, the domain decomposition that maps the requirements
of this chapter onto bounded contexts, and the deployment topology
that hosts them. Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation} then consolidates the
realised platform, and Chapter~\ref{chap:future}
sets out the improvements to the event-sourced system that the
remainder of the internship is devoted to.
