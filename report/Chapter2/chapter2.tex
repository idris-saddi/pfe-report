\section*{Introduction}
This chapter specifies what CLMPilot is required to do. We start from
the users: we first catalogue the actors who interact with the
platform. From the problem statement and the actors we then derive the
functional requirements, stated as capabilities, not implementation
detail, and the non-functional requirements: the qualities the platform
must exhibit while delivering them. We close by expanding the use cases
the actors initiate, from a single high-level overview down to the
individual cases of each functional area, with the most important
scenarios described step by step.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Actors}
\label{sec:actors}

The actor model is intentionally narrow. CLMPilot is a multi-tenant
platform: each customer organisation is an isolated tenant, so the
catalogue below centres on the roles \emph{within} a single tenant,
mapping onto the functions that already exist in a Mittelstand
legal-operations team. Outside the tenant, a platform-level
\emph{Super~Admin} provisions and isolates the tenants themselves; that
role belongs to the operator rather than the customer, and lies outside
the scope of this project's contribution, but it is catalogued here so
the actor model is complete.

\subsection{Actor catalogue}
\label{sec:actors-catalogue}

Table~\ref{tab:actors} catalogues the 4 per-tenant actors, together
with the platform-level Super~Admin, and the use cases each one
initiates.

2 non-human actors complete the catalogue. The system clock is the
trigger for the scheduled use cases of the deadline-management
subsystem; we treat it separately to make the provenance of
system-initiated actions explicit in the audit log. And the LLM (the
intelligence service) is an external secondary actor: it supports the
intelligence-assisted capture of contract metadata and clauses when a
document is linked.

\section{Functional requirements}
\label{sec:functional-requirements}

The functional requirements that follow flow directly from the 4 pain
points of the problem statement. They are stated as outcomes, not mechanisms:
each subsection identifies the actor that initiates
the use case, the outcome the platform must produce, and the constraints
the surrounding context imposes. The architectural decomposition into
domains, commands and events is deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}.

\subsection{Contract registry}
The contract registry is the platform's central object and the entry
point for every other capability. The Legal~Manager creates a contract
record by supplying the counterparty, the contract category, the start
and end dates, the notice window and the contract value. The same actor
edits these attributes over the contract's lifetime and decommissions
the record by archiving rather than deleting it: the immutability
requirement of Section~\ref{sec:nfr-audit} forbids hard deletion. The
registry supports filtering by status, category, business unit and
counterparty, full-text search on free-form fields, and pagination over
the result set. Performance targets for these operations are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:nfr-perf}.

\subsection{Document linking}
A contract record is connected to the document that embodies it
through a \textit{link} -- a typed reference to a file held in the
customer's own document store (Google~Drive, SharePoint, or any
HTTP-addressable location). The Legal~Manager attaches and detaches
links; the platform resolves them at display time, showing the file
name, last-modified timestamp and a click-through to the original
location. The platform never copies the document contents into its own
storage: the integration-first stance of
Section~\ref{sec:competitive-landscape} is enforced at this boundary.

\subsection{Deadline and renewal management}
Every contract carries up to 3 temporal markers: its expiry date,
the start of its notice window, and, where applicable, its
auto-renewal date. The platform must highlight each of these markers
before it passes, through a reminder workflow that emits notifications
at 90, 60 and 30~days before the notice window opens, and again on the
day the window closes. The notification target is the contract's
designated owner, by default the Legal~Manager who created the record.
The reminder schedule is generated by the platform itself: the actor
who triggers the use case is the system clock, not a human user.

\subsection{Approval workflows}
A contract under negotiation moves through a sequence of approvals
before it is signed. The Legal~Manager initiates an approval request
and selects a workflow template: a sequence of approval steps, each
addressed to one or several Approvers. Each Approver receives a request
and answers with one of 3 decisions: approve, reject, or request
changes. A reject or request-changes outcome interrupts the workflow
and returns the contract to the initiator; an approval advances it to
the next step. The platform records every decision, its author, its
timestamp and any comment, and these records are not editable after
the fact.

\subsection{Compliance and audit trail}
Every action that changes the state of a contract (creation, edit,
link attachment, approval decision, archiving) is recorded in an
immutable audit log. The Auditor accesses the log read-only and exports
it in a format independent of the application: a tabular file usable in
spreadsheet software, with one row per recorded action, and columns for
the actor, the timestamp, the contract identifier and the action type.
The log must satisfy the GoBD retention requirement of 10 years and
must be reproducible at any date within that window, even after the
underlying business state has changed.

\subsection{Dashboard and reporting}
The dashboard is the Legal~Manager's daily view. It shows the
portfolio at a glance: counts of active, expired and pending contracts;
the contracts whose notice windows open in the next 30, 60 and 90~days;
the approval requests awaiting the user's decision; and the aggregate
value of the active portfolio. Beyond the standing dashboard, the
platform supports parametric reports (contracts by counterparty,
spend by quarter, renewals by business unit) to answer the portfolio
questions raised in the problem statement.

\subsection{Identity and access management}
Identity and access management (IAM) gates every other capability.
The Admin creates user accounts, assigns roles and revokes
access; users authenticate with an email address, a password and a
second factor delivered as a one-time code by email. The platform enforces role-based access
control along the actor catalogue of
Section~\ref{sec:actors-catalogue}: the same query can return different
projections depending on the caller's role, and any command that
exceeds the caller's authority is refused before it reaches the domain.
The reset, invitation and lockout flows are required at this layer
because they are the flows through which non-Admin users actually
exercise IAM.

\subsection{Advanced capabilities extending the core registry}
\label{sec:fr-enterprise}
Beyond the core registry, the platform is required to support a tier of
enterprise capabilities that address the same 4 pain
points more fully. Document linking is expected to trigger \textit{intelligence-assisted
capture}: when a contract's document is attached, the platform proposes
counterparty, dates, value and detected clauses for the Legal~Manager to
confirm, rather than leaving every field to manual entry. The platform must
take a contract through \textit{electronic signature} with an external
counterparty and reflect the signed outcome on the record, so that execution
is part of the tracked lifecycle.
\textit{Per-tenant custom fields} let each organisation extend the contract
schema to its own taxonomy without a code change. \textit{Post-signature
obligation tracking} extends standard deadline management so the
organisation tracks the commitments a signed contract creates (payments,
reviews, renewals) for as long as it runs. And \textit{advanced, schedulable
reporting} answers the portfolio questions of the problem statement on a
recurring basis rather than on demand. These capabilities are stated here
as requirements; how they were built is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chap:realisation}, and Chapter~\ref{chap:delivered} shows
them in the delivered product.

\section{Non-functional requirements}

Non-functional requirements describe the qualities the platform must
exhibit while delivering the functional capabilities of the previous
section. We group them along 7 axes (security, compliance,
availability, performance, auditability, internationalisation and
maintainability), each justified by a constraint of the deployment
context.

\subsection{Security}
\label{sec:nfr-security}
All traffic between the client and the platform is carried over
Transport-Layer Security (TLS) in its current widely-deployed version;
data at rest in the event store and the relational projections is
encrypted with a key under the operator's control. Every command and
query is authenticated; authorisation is enforced before the request
reaches the domain layer, on the basis of the caller's role. Passwords
are stored as salted hashes computed with a modern key-derivation
function. A second factor of authentication, a one-time code delivered by
email, is mandatory for Admin accounts and offered to every other
role.

\subsection{Compliance and data residency}
\label{sec:nfr-compliance}
The platform must satisfy 3 regulatory baselines simultaneously.
GoBD requires that contract records be retained immutably and
recoverable in their state as of any past date, for a period of up to
10 years. The GDPR requires that
personal data be processed under documented purposes, subject to access
and erasure requests, and stored within the European Union. ISO~27001
imposes a documented information-security management system, with
access controls, change tracking and incident response procedures. The
production environment is hosted in the European Union by default,
under a contract with an EU-resident provider; no customer data is
processed or stored outside the EU without an explicit opt-in.

\subsection{Availability and reliability}
The minimum viable product (MVP) carries an availability target rather
than a contractually committed Service-Level Objective (SLO):
99.5\% monthly during DACH business hours and
99.0\% outside them, to be validated against operational
data once production traffic is observed. Recovery-Point Objective
(RPO) is set at 24 hours, sustained by nightly off-site
backups; Recovery-Time Objective (RTO) at 4 hours. These figures
are MVP targets and will be tightened or relaxed based on customer
commitments in later phases. The platform degrades gracefully when
external document stores are unreachable: contract metadata and
workflow remain available, and document previews show a
transient-error state rather than failing the surrounding page.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:nfr-perf}
The performance budget for the MVP is expressed as engineering
targets to be measured and tuned against, not as contractual
guarantees. On a contemporary EU broadband connection, median
page-load latency aims for under 1 second on the dashboard and the
contract list; search and filter operations over a portfolio of 10{,}000
contracts aim for under 500 milliseconds; and
document-link resolution (the round-trip to the external store,
plus the metadata fetch) aims for under 2 seconds in the median
case. Failure modes degrade to the graceful state described above.
Table~\ref{tab:perf-metrics} states these budgets as the metrics
against which the performance tests measure the platform; each row can
be exercised by an automated test against a seeded environment. The
availability and recovery objectives of the previous subsection are
not testable this way: they are validated operationally, through the
uptime probe and the periodic restore drill.

\subsection{Auditability}
\label{sec:nfr-audit}
Every state-changing action in the platform is recorded as an immutable,
time-stamped, actor-attributed event. There is no primitive for
in-place update or destructive delete at the storage layer: a correction
is itself an event, layered on top of the prior history. The audit log
is exportable in a tabular format independent of the application's
runtime and is reconstructible at any point in time within the GoBD
retention window. This requirement aligns directly with the principle of
compliance by architecture and drives the architectural choices
presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:arch}.

\subsection{Internationalisation}
\label{sec:nfr-i18n}
English is the primary language of the user interface, with German
maintained alongside it; other languages can be added later, with
translation slots reserved in the user-interface
layer from the outset. Dates, numbers and currencies are formatted
according to the user's declared locale rather than the server's,
and the platform stores time-stamps in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)
and renders them in the user's time zone. Email notifications inherit
the recipient's locale.

\subsection{Maintainability}
The platform was built by a single developer during a brief internship, so
every architectural choice had to minimise the long-term maintenance burden.
The platform relies on a narrow, well-understood technology
stack rather than a fragmented mix of specialised tools.
To keep the project manageable for future developers, it incorporates
conventional commit messages, a predictable release-train cadence
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), and 3 distinct layers of automated
testing (unit, integration, and end-to-end). Finally, documentation is stored
directly alongside the code and compiled straight into the running application's
help system, preventing the documentation from drifting out of date.

\section*{Conclusion}
This chapter has specified what the platform must do and how well it
must do it. We catalogued the actors: 4 per-tenant human roles
specialising a common user, the platform-level Super~Admin outside this
project's scope, and the system clock and the LLM as non-human actors.
From the problem statement we derived the functional requirements, from
the core contract registry to the tier of enterprise capabilities, and
the 7 axes of non-functional requirements that constrain how they
must be delivered. We then expanded the use cases the actors initiate
from a single high-level overview down to the individual cases of each
functional area, describing the contract-creation, approval and
audit-reconstruction scenarios step by step.

With the requirements specified, Chapter~\ref{chap:arch} presents the
structural response: the architecture through which these requirements
are satisfied by construction.
